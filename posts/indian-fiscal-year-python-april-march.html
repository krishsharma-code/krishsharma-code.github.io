Your date.year Is Wrong for a Quarter of India's Calendar

25 November 2026 · Krish Sharma

Take an invoice dated 20 February 2026. Now ask Python what year it belongs to. date(2026, 2, 20).year answers 2026, and for an Indian business that answer is wrong — the invoice sits inside FY 2025-26, the same fiscal year as a receipt from 15 May 2025.

That is a one-line bug that survives review, because the code looks correct. Nothing crashes. The totals are just quietly filed under the wrong year, and the error only surfaces when someone reconciles a GST return in July.

The problem: one quarter of the calendar does not exist

India's financial year runs from 1 April to 31 March. Every annual report, every GST filing period, every TDS quarter, every payroll statement is keyed to that boundary. Standard library datetime knows nothing about it, so each project reinvents the same three lines of month arithmetic — usually slightly differently, and usually wrong at the edges.

I am Krish Sharma (krishsharma-code), and my daily-dev-workspace repo is where I keep one self-contained piece of work per feature. This week's addition is python/fiscal-year: a dependency-free module that answers the five questions that come up whenever dates meet Indian reporting. Standard library only — datetime and re, nothing else.

How it works

The core idea is that a fiscal year is identified by the calendar year it starts in. FY 2025-26 opens on 1 April 2025 and closes on 31 March 2026, so both of those dates return 2025. That single decision makes every label and every range consistent.

Here is the real implementation from fiscal_year.py, the two functions everything else builds on:

def fy_of(value):
    """Return the start year of the fiscal year containing ``value``.

    >>> fy_of(date(2025, 4, 1))
    2025
    >>> fy_of(date(2026, 3, 31))
    2025
    """
    day = _as_date(value)
    return day.year if day.month >= _FY_START_MONTH else day.year - 1


def quarter_bounds(year, quarter):
    """Return ``(first_day, last_day)`` of a fiscal quarter, inclusive.

    >>> quarter_bounds(2025, 4)
    (datetime.date(2026, 1, 1), datetime.date(2026, 3, 31))
    """
    year = _check_year(year)
    quarter = _check_quarter(quarter)
    open_month = _QUARTER_OPEN_MONTH[quarter]
    open_year = year if open_month >= _FY_START_MONTH else year + 1
    start = _dt.date(open_year, open_month, 1)
    # The last day of a quarter is the day before the next one opens, which
    # keeps leap years and 31-day months correct without a table.
    close_month = open_month + 3
    if close_month > 12:
        end = _dt.date(open_year + 1, close_month - 12, 1) - _dt.timedelta(days=1)
    else:
        end = _dt.date(open_year, close_month, 1) - _dt.timedelta(days=1)
    return (start, end)

That comment in quarter_bounds is the part I would keep in any rewrite. The obvious implementation is a table of month lengths with a special case for February. Computing the last day as "the day before the next quarter opens" removes the table and the special case together, so a leap year cannot fall through a gap.

Quarters are fiscal, not calendar: Q1 is Apr-Jun, Q2 Jul-Sep, Q3 Oct-Dec, Q4 Jan-Mar. So quarter_of(date(2026, 1, 5)) returns 4, not 1 — the mistake most reporting code makes when someone finally notices the year is wrong but not the quarter.

What's new in this release

The module ships fourteen public functions plus one exception type, all in a single 364-line file:

  • Lookups — fy_of(), fy_bounds(), quarter_of(), quarter_bounds(), month_of_fy().
  • Labels — fy_label() in four styles ('FY2025-26', 'FY2025-2026', '2025-26', 'FY25') and quarter_label() for strings like 'Q4 FY2025-26'.
  • Parsing — parse_fy() reads the labels people actually type: FY 2025-26, 2025-2026, 2025/26, FY 25, even a bare 2025. En and em dashes, stray spaces and mixed case are all tolerated.
  • Arithmetic — shift_fy(), fy_months(), fy_quarters(), fy_days() and fy_span().

The behaviour that took the most thought is refusal. parse_fy('2025-2027') raises instead of guessing which year you meant, and so do '2025-25' and '26-25'. Passing a string, a float or None where a date belongs raises FiscalYearError, a ValueError subclass, rather than being coerced. A datetime is accepted and truncated to its day.

Two documented limits are deliberate. Two-digit labels are read as 20xx, so 'FY99' means 2099 — a compact label carries no century, and pretending otherwise would be a guess. Years are bounded to 1000-9998 so that every label and the 31 March closing the year stay representable.

The module comes with a runnable example.py that buckets a ledger of eight invoices across the 1 April boundary and parses a list of messy imported labels, and a test suite of 121 tests that all pass, plus doctested examples in the docstrings. That is the repo's rule: a subfolder ships with its own code, its own tests and its own README, or it does not get committed.

Roadmap

Nothing below exists yet — it is where I think this goes next, not a promise:

  • A thin adapter so a dict or csv export can be grouped by fiscal year without a hand-written loop in every script.
  • An optional start_month parameter, since a few countries and a few internal calendars do not open in April.
  • Property-based tests for the boundary pairs, so 1 April and 31 March are proven exhaustive rather than spot-checked.
  • Type hints verified under mypy --strict in CI, the same gate I wired into my internship repo.

Conclusion

Every one of these bugs is the same bug: date.year answering a calendar question when the business asked a fiscal one. It costs three lines to write a helper, and it costs a week to find the mistake in a filed return.

If you write Python that touches Indian money, dates or reporting, take the module. It has no dependencies, so it drops into a script or a service without a lockfile conversation.
